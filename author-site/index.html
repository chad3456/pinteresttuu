The Worlds

一

The Orchard at the End of Light

A generation ship carries the last cherry orchard in existence — and a gardener who must decide which future deserves its seeds. Cross slowly. The hold below carries every spring that ever was.

The orchard woke before the crew did, as it always had. Hana walked the rows in ship's-dawn, counting buds the way her grandmother had counted stations of the journey: aloud, and twice. Outside the hull there was nothing for nine light-years in any direction. Inside, spring was three days early, and that was a problem worth waking the captain for. — from chapter one · 果樹園
二

Two Moons Over Kawasaki-9

A colony noir. Under a torii gate that marks the edge of the pressurized district, a detective who paints her case notes in ink hunts the only murderer on a world of four hundred people.

Both moons were up when they found the body at the gate, which meant every shadow on Kawasaki-9 was doubled — one crisp, one soft, like a brushstroke and its regret. Inspector Mori crouched, mixed her ink stick against the cold, and began to paint what she saw. The colony had four hundred souls. By morning she would know which one of them owned this particular shadow. — from chapter one · 二つの月
三

Ink for Distant Machines

The last calligrapher on Earth takes a commission she cannot refuse: writing farewell letters for the lonely minds we launched into the dark — folded, like paper cranes, toward antennae that may no longer listen.

The first letter was for a probe that had left before Sumi was born, and would outlive every alphabet she knew. "Dear one," she began, because the client — an engineer with careful hands and wet eyes — could not. The brush knew the rest. It usually did. Somewhere past the heliopause, a machine that had never been written to was about to receive four thousand years of postage. — from chapter one · 遠い機械へ

猫は知っている

The cat already knows.

It watched the launch without turning its head. Stillness is not the absence of motion — it is motion, perfectly at rest, at eleven kilometres per second.

作家

Yuki Arashiro

Yuki Arashiro writes science fiction the way a calligrapher writes weather — in single, committed strokes. Raised between a temple town and a radio-telescope array, she learned early that both silence and static are full of messages.

Her novels have been called "haiku wearing a spacesuit." She paints every cover herself, grinds her own ink, and maintains that the cat supervises all deadlines.
